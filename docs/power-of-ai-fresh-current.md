## 9. Why the mix-up costs you
*content slide · label: What is AI? · 04 · 3 clicks · FACT CHECK*

**On the slide:** Why the mix-up costs you · 01 · Wrong tool, wrong job · Chatbots are bad at exact sums, forecasts and strict look-ups. Force them and the errors pile up. · 02 · AI you pay for but never switched on · Accounting, CRM and shop software often has AI built in. **Notes:** Three reasons, one click each. ¶ 1. Using the wrong tool for the job: when a firm thinks AI equals a chatbot, staff force large language models to do tasks they are bad at, such as deterministic accounting, stock forecasting or strict database look-ups. Frustration and errors follow. ¶ 2. Overlooking built-in tools: many UK SMEs already pay for predictive or automation AI inside platforms such as Xero, Sage, HubSpot or Shopify without switching those features on, because they assume AI means buying a separate chatbot licence. ¶ 3. Misjudging risk and governance: a chatbot hallucinating a marketing blog post is low risk; an autonomous agent or a predictive credit-scoring model making decisions on customer data carries serious UK GDPR and compliance implications.

## 20. Why people stall
*content slide · label: The UK picture · 06 · 4 clicks*

**On the slide:** Learning feels like this. · Experience · Time · Looks like fun. · This is hard! · No idea what I’m doing. But I keep going. · Now I get it! · …or not. · I know nothing! · This actually makes sense. · Did I do that? · I did it!

**Notes:** Learning a new tool is not a straight line up, and this is why so many firms stall (NatWest found 29% of firms using AI still in the earliest piloting stage). ¶ Walk the curve, a click at a time. "Looks like fun": the first quick wins. "This is hard!": the first dip. "No idea what I’m doing, but I keep going": the climb. "Now I get it!" ... "or not": the novelty wears off right as the real work begins. "I know nothing!": the low point, where most people quit. Then "This actually makes sense", "Did I do that?", and "I did it!". ¶ The point: the low point is normal, and it comes before fluency, not instead of it. The usual explanation is the brain’s novelty reward (the buzz of a quick win) fading once the tool feels familiar; that is popular psychology, so offer it as a way of thinking, not as a research finding.

## 21. Four reasons the buzz wears off
*content slide · label: The UK picture · 07 · 4 clicks*

**On the slide:** Four reasons the buzz wears off · The novelty cliff · New features feel like quick wins. Once the tool feels familiar, the buzz stops. · The competency dip · The basics come fast. The tricky real cases come slowly, and that gap is frustrating. · Setup feels like progress · Installing and arranging the tool feels productive. Then you face the hard task it was for. · Too many settings · Endless tweaking drains the energy you needed for the real work.

**Notes:** One click each. ¶ The novelty cliff: exploring new features gives easy rewards; once the interface feels familiar, that reward stops. ¶ The competency dip: learning the basics feels fast, mastering the complex edge cases feels slow, and the gap between knowing how a tool works and being fluent with it causes frustration. ¶ Setup feels like progress ("tool-shifting procrastination"): setting up a new app or workflow feels like productive work; once it is done, you have to face the actual, often harder task the tool was meant to solve. ¶ Too many settings (decision fatigue): new tools come with endless customisation; spending energy tweaking settings drains the willpower needed for real work. ¶ Ask the room: "Hands up if you’ve spent longer setting up a tool than using it."

## 30. Most of the time, the agent is waiting
*content slide · label: Over the horizon · 03 · 1 clicks*

**On the slide:** Most of the time, the agent is waiting for you. · ~80% · of a session, waiting for a person · the rest · Reading everything their agents write would take over half the working week, even for non-technical staff. · Source: Anthropic, How employment is changing at Anthropic (16 October 2026)

**Notes:** The bottleneck has moved. Around 80% of a Claude session is spent idly waiting for human input: review, clarification, approval. **On the slide:** Read it with care. · The authors’ own caveats · An unusual company · Much of what happens there can’t be copied across the economy. · Activity isn’t value · More code, more agent hours: not the same as better work. · Errors hide better · AI mistakes may be harder to spot, so quality checks can miss them. · Growth muddies it · The company grew fast at the same time, which explains some of the gain.

## 36. What it means for your team
*content slide · label: Over the horizon · 09 · 4 clicks*

**On the slide:** What it means for your team · Checking becomes the bottleneck · Batch approvals and automate the routine checks, or seniors drown in review queues. · Watch for rubber-stamping · A flooded, tired reviewer waves subtle errors through. Check less, but check properly. · Rethink the first job · Routine entry-level work is shrinking. Hire for judgement and problem framing, and keep growing juniors. · Write the rules once · Prompt templates, brand rules and checklists, not one message at a time.

**Notes:** What a small team takes from Anthropic’s report: an early warning not to confuse agent activity with productivity. ¶ Your bottleneck shifts from doing to checking: deploying agents will not cure a small team’s workload if every output needs a manual sign-off; batch the approvals and automate the routine quality checks. ¶ Protect against rubber-stamp risk: a lean team flooded with AI-made proposals, financial models or code gets review fatigue fast, and tired people approve subtle errors. ¶ Rethink entry-level roles: routine execution work is shrinking, so future hires need judgement and problem-framing skills to direct the tools. Small firms still need to grow their own people, because the checker of tomorrow is today’s junior. ¶ Invest in guardrails, "meta-work": Anthropic’s staff answered the surge with standing instructions and automated checks; for an SME that means reusable prompt templates, brand guardrails and checklists, not managing AI one message at a time.
